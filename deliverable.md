## 2. Funding Mechanisms for Each Loyalty Program

### Marriott International — Marriott Bonvoy

Marriott Bonvoy is funded through a **multi-layered, externalized model** with three principal funding streams:

1. **Co-Brand Credit Card Agreements (Dominant Source):**
   - Marriott has long-term agreements with JPMorgan Chase and American Express. These financial institutions issue co-brand credit cards bearing the Marriott Bonvoy name.
   - Marriott receives: (a) **royalty fees** based on cardmember spending and new account openings, (b) revenue from **selling Marriott Bonvoy points** to the banks (which the banks provide to cardmembers as a benefit of card usage), and (c) marketing and program support fees.
   - Per the Q3 2025 earnings call, **co-brand card payments account for more than half of the Marriott Bonvoy program funding.**
   - In 2020, Marriott received $920 million in cash from co-brand partners via amendments that included prepayments of future revenues and a signing bonus, illustrating the significant cash-flow-generating power of this funding stream.

2. **Cost Reimbursements from Hotel Owners:**
   - Both managed and franchised hotels are assessed charges for centralized programs and services, including the Loyalty Program, reservations, and marketing.
   - These are recorded as "Cost Reimbursement Revenue" (and the associated expenses as "Reimbursed Expenses"). This is a pass-through mechanism that does not generate profit but ensures the program's operating costs are covered by the properties that benefit.

3. **Breakage Revenue:**
   - When loyalty points expire or are forecast not to be redeemed, Marriott recognizes breakage, which flows through as fee revenue.

**Key Structural Advantage:** Because the co-brand banks pre-purchase points and pay ongoing royalties based on *their cardholders' total spend* (not just hotel spend), a significant portion of Marriott Bonvoy funding comes from **outside the hotel P&L** — from the consumer credit card ecosystem. This means Marriott can invest more in the program's value proposition (elite benefits, promotions, partnerships) without directly burdening hotel owner economics.

---

### Wyndham Hotels & Resorts — Wyndham Rewards

Wyndham Rewards is funded through a **simpler, hotel-contribution model**:

1. **Hotel Contributions from Eligible Revenues:**
   - The program "is funded by contributions from eligible revenues generated by Wyndham Rewards members and collected by us from hotels in our system."
   - These funds are applied to: (a) reimburse hotels and partners for Wyndham Rewards points redemptions by loyalty members, (b) pay for administrative expenses, and (c) fund marketing initiatives that support the program.
   - This is fundamentally a **self-funded, closed-loop system** — hotels pay in, and the program pays out.

2. **Co-Brand Credit Card Program (Secondary):**
   - Wyndham does have a co-branded credit card program (referenced in the 10-K deferred-revenue tables), but it is far smaller in scale and not described as a primary funding mechanism.
   - Partnership fees from co-brand and other ancillary sources are captured within "Marketing, Reservation, and Loyalty" revenue and "Other Products and Services."

3. **Marketing and Reservation Fees:**
   - Separate from the loyalty fund, Wyndham collects marketing and reservation fees from hotels, some of which support loyalty-related marketing.

**Key Structural Feature:** The Wyndham Rewards funding model is predominantly **zero-margin / pass-through**, with limited external funding. The program's scale and benefit richness are directly constrained by what the system's hotels generate from member stays.

## 3. Comparative Assessment: Which Company Is Better Positioned to Translate Loyalty Strength Into Durable Economic Advantage?

### Marriott International (MAR) is significantly better positioned.

The conclusion rests on four dimensions:

#### A. Penetration Depth and Breadth

Marriott Bonvoy commands **75% U.S. / 68% global** member penetration of room nights versus Wyndham Rewards' **53% U.S. / 37% global**. This ~15-31 percentage point gap means Marriott's loyalty program captures a far greater share of its system's demand, making it more indispensable to both guests and hotel owners. Critically, Marriott's global penetration (68%) is only 7 points below its U.S. figure, demonstrating consistent engagement worldwide — a key advantage given Marriott's 145-country footprint. Wyndham's global penetration at 37% suggests its program is far less embedded outside the U.S.

#### B. Funding Model: Externalized vs. Self-Funded

This is the decisive structural difference. Marriott Bonvoy's reliance on **co-brand credit card economics** — where more than half of program funding comes from banks paying for points and royalties on *total cardholder spend* — creates a virtuous flywheel:

- More engaged members → more co-brand card sign-ups and spend → higher bank royalties and point sales → more resources to invest in the program's value proposition (elite tiers, experiential rewards, marketing) → deeper member engagement → repeat.

By contrast, Wyndham Rewards is predominantly funded by assessments on its own hotels' revenues from member stays — a **closed loop**. Every dollar spent enriching the program must ultimately be recovered from hotel owner contributions, creating a natural tension between program investment and franchisee economics. Wyndham's lower price-point positioning (economy/midscale) further constrains the absolute dollar pool available.

#### C. Pricing Power and Brand Portfolio Leverage

Marriott's portfolio spans Luxury (Ritz-Carlton, St. Regis, Edition) through Midscale (City Express, Four Points Flex), giving its loyalty program currency across a vast range of price points and trip purposes. A member can earn points on a $600/night Ritz-Carlton stay and redeem them at a $120/night Fairfield Inn — or vice versa. This **internal redemption ecosystem** locks members into the Marriott system.

Wyndham's portfolio, while large, is concentrated in economy and midscale brands where average daily rates are far lower (global RevPAR of ~$43 vs. Marriott's significantly higher blended rate). Lower room rates mean fewer points earned per stay, which limits aspirational redemption value and reduces the program's "pull" as a demand driver.

#### D. Owner/Franchisee Value Proposition

Marriott's co-brand funding model means the loyalty program delivers **incremental demand to hotels that is partly subsidized by external parties** (banks and their cardholders). Hotel owners see the program as a net benefit — it drives occupancy at a lower effective cost than alternative marketing channels.

Wyndham's self-funded model means franchisees more directly bear the cost of the program. While Wyndham Rewards is consistently rated as one of the simplest and most generous programs (e.g., flat 7,500/15,000/30,000 points per night redemptions), the economic burden on owners can limit program evolution and create friction.

### Conclusion

Marriott International is better positioned to translate loyalty-program strength into durable economic advantage. The combination of (i) materially higher member penetration in both the U.S. and globally, (ii) a funding model that harnesses external co-brand credit card economics rather than solely taxing hotel owner P&Ls, and (iii) a brand portfolio that spans the full pricing spectrum gives Marriott Bonvoy the characteristics of a self-reinforcing economic moat. Wyndham Rewards, while well-regarded for simplicity and member value, operates with a structurally constrained funding model and lower global engagement — limiting its ability to compound advantage over time.
